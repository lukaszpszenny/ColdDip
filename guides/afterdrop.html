What is afterdrop?

By Łukasz Pszenny, an ice swimmer from Bydgoszcz, Poland · Updated 26 Nov 2026

Many people are surprised that the coldest moment often comes not in the water but a few minutes after getting out. That is usually afterdrop.

What happens

In cold water your body pulls blood away from the skin, arms and legs to protect the core. After you get out, the blood vessels open again and cooler blood from the skin and limbs flows back. As a result, the temperature inside your body can keep dropping for a while after the dip — often for 10 to 30 minutes, sometimes longer after a long dip.

Signs to watch for

  • shivering that starts or gets stronger after you are already dressed,
  • clumsy fingers, trouble with zips and laces,
  • feeling dizzy, unusually tired or confused.

Shivering is normal and helps you warm up. Confusion, fainting or shivering that will not settle are reasons to ask for help.

How to warm up calmly

  1. Prepare before you go in. Lay out a towel and clothes in the order you put them on. A hat, gloves and warm shoes help a lot.
  2. Dry off and dress quickly. Upper body first, then the rest. Get out of the wind.
  3. Warm up from the inside. A warm (not boiling) sweet drink and a snack help.
  4. Move gently. Walking or light movement helps you produce heat.
  5. Go easy on very hot showers or a sauna straight away. Many clubs advise warming up gradually first, because sudden heat can make you feel faint.
  6. Wait before you drive or cycle. Do not get behind the wheel until you have stopped shivering and really feel well.

Keep dips shorter to keep afterdrop smaller

The longer and colder the dip, the stronger the afterdrop usually is. That is one reason to keep first dips short — see how long to stay in cold water.

How Cold Dip helps

After you press the top button to leave the water, Cold Dip switches to the “After exit” stage: it counts the time since you got out and shows an afterdrop (warm-up) countdown, so you can see how long you have been warming up. The app does not measure your body temperature — no watch can.

These are general tips from an ice swimmer, not medical advice. If you feel unwell after a dip, ask for help. Never dip alone.
